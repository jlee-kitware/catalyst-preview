Something in your workflow decides what to throw away.

Usually a number in a config file. Write every hundredth timestep, because that is what the filesystem will take, and hope the one you needed was not among the other ninety-nine.

Catalyst does the analysis while the data is still in memory: on the solver’s own nodes, streamed to neighbouring ones, or both. Never by way of the filesystem. So instead of picking which timesteps to lose, you pick what to measure, and you get it for every one of them.

Why that config number is small: on Titan, compute bandwidth was 125 PB/s against 1.4 TB/s to storage. A gap near 89,000 : 1, and widening. Moreland, IEEE CG&A 36(2), 2016, Fig. 1

Where these numbers come from. The published WarpX timestep is a 5.75 billion element mesh at 1.16 TB, so N ≈ 1,792 on a side. Twenty integrated quantities are 160 bytes. A 1920×1080 frame is about 2 MB whatever the mesh size. A slice is N² = 3.2 million cells, and as data with six fields at single precision that is roughly 0.18 GB. Writing everything is 1.16 TB. Six orders of magnitude separate the first from the last, and the choice is yours, not the filesystem's. Only the last option registers on the lower bar. The other three are drawn as a marker because at this scale they are a rounding error, which is the entire argument.

This is not a choice between in situ visualization and post-processing. You may still write files, still load them in ParaView, still explore them afterwards. In situ is allows you to decide what gets extracted and which files, so what lands on disk is your say.

You link a stub. ParaView loads at run time.

Three function calls and one header. Your solver describes the mesh it already has and hands over a pointer; ParaView is not in your build system, not in your dependency tree, and not in your support matrix.

The pipeline is Python, with the full VTK and ParaView APIs in scope, so a new extract, plot or derived quantity is a script rather than a build. The same adaptor serves analysis on the solver’s own ranks, on separate nodes, or both at once.

Adaptors exist in C, C++, Fortran and Python. Converting LULESH to this API cut it from 325 lines to 155. how, and what it costs →

/* the entire integration surface */
#include <catalyst.h>

catalyst_initialize(params);   // once
catalyst_execute(params);      // when you choose
catalyst_finalize(params);     // once

Fields are passed by pointer. Catalyst reads your arrays in place, and GPU-resident data stays on the GPU.

If you sell simulation software, the argument is different.

Post-processing is a permanent line item on your roadmap and it never stops asking for headcount. Ship one adaptor and it becomes an extension point: your customers write their own analysis, in Python, on their own schedule.

New capability arrives without a new release. You can hand a customer an extract or a derived quantity mid support cycle without recompiling, without a regression run, and without disturbing a binary they have already validated.

Two ways in, and one of them needs nothing from us.

Catalyst is free and the adaptor is small enough that plenty of teams instrument their own solver without ever talking to us. A graduate student did PLUTO in 273 lines.

What takes longer to learn is which pipeline answers your question, what it costs at your concurrency, and where it stops scaling. That is what the assessment is for: a fixed-fee review of your solver, delivering an adaptor architecture and an effort estimate before you commit to anything larger.
