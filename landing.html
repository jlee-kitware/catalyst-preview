The simulation ends. The waiting begins.

Solvers got orders of magnitude faster over the last decade and their output grew with them. Everything after stayed exactly where it was: write it all out, move it, read it back, post-process, and only then look. That stretch is now most of the distance between a run and a design decision.

In situ analysis removes the middle of it. Catalyst runs your analysis inside the solve, so the results are there while the run is still going.

Why the round trip costs a week

A WarpX timestep on Frontier produces a 5.75 billion element mesh, 1.16 TB. Post-hoc, all of it has to land on disk before you can look at any of it, and then be read back again to do the analysis. In situ, only the results you asked for are ever written.

Where these numbers come from. The published WarpX timestep is a 5.75 billion element mesh at 1.16 TB, so N ≈ 1,792 on a side. Twenty integrated quantities are 160 bytes. A 1920×1080 frame is about 2 MB whatever the mesh size. A slice is N² = 3.2 million cells, and as data with six fields at single precision that is roughly 0.18 GB. Writing everything is 1.16 TB. Six orders of magnitude separate the first from the last, and the choice is yours, not the filesystem's. Only the last option registers on the lower bar. The other three are drawn as a marker because at this scale they are a rounding error, which is the entire argument.

This is not a choice between in situ visualization and post-processing. You may still write files, still load them in ParaView, still explore them afterwards. In situ lets you decide what gets extracted and which files, so what lands on disk is your say.

And you can start without touching your solver at all. A file-based adaptor runs the same pipelines against data you already have, so an existing archive can be mined this afternoon with the analysis you were going to write anyway. Whatever you build against yesterday’s files is the same thing that runs inside tomorrow’s solve, unchanged. Most people do not know Catalyst does this, and it is the cheapest way to find out whether any of the rest is worth it.

However you work, it is the same middle you are waiting on.

Steering a live run, sweeping a design space, leaving a job unattended, training a model on the output, or going back through an archive you already have. Different workflows, one bottleneck, one place to remove it.

Watch it, and change it

Attach to a running solve, catch a diverging case in hour four of three days, and steer a parameter from what the analysis just found. The correction does not wait for the queue.

Run hundreds, compare all of them

Ensembles and design sweeps where full output per member is out of the question. Every member emits its quantities of interest at full temporal fidelity, so the comparison exists the moment the last run does.

Let it decide when to look

Trigger on a detected feature, keep only what matters, and leave the job unattended. Nobody has to watch a screen for three days to catch the one thing worth catching.

Train on it as it goes

Surrogates and reduced-order models read the runtime stream directly, so the training set is a by-product of the campaign rather than a second project with its own storage budget.
